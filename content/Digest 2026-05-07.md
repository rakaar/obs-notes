## Paper 1: Belief Dynamics Reveal the Dual Nature of In-Context Learning and Activation Steering

**Authors:** Eric Bigelow, Daniel Wurgaft, YingQiao Wang, Noah Goodman, Tomer Ullman, Hidenori Tanaka, Ekdeep Singh Lubana  
**Venue:** arXiv 2025 (cs.LG) — ICML-related  
**Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.00617  
**Note:** [[2026/causality, prediction in neuro, ai]]  
**Access:** ✅ Full PDF fetched from arXiv

---

### a) Introduction

This paper addresses a fundamental question: are in-context learning (ICL) and activation steering fundamentally different mechanisms, or instances of the same underlying process? Both are inference-time control methods for LLMs—ICL modifies inputs (prompts), while steering modifies internal activations. The authors argue they are unified under a single Bayesian framework: both methods alter the model's **belief in latent concepts**. ICL accumulates evidence (likelihood), while steering changes priors.

**Why it matters:** If true, this provides a predictive theory for combining prompts and steering—practically useful for AI safety and model control.

---

### b) Main Contribution

The core idea is a Bayesian belief dynamics model:

- **ICL as evidence accumulation:** Adding in-context examples updates the posterior p(c|x) ∝ p(x|c)p(c). The likelihood p(x|c) scales sub-linearly with N (number of examples), following N^(1-α).
- **Steering as prior intervention:** Activation steering along a concept direction d_i linearly shifts log-prior odds: log[p(c_i|v+m·d_i)/p(c'_i|v+m·d_i)] = aβ_i(v) + a·m + b

The closed-form model predicts:
1. Sigmoidal learning curves in ICL (as function of N^(1-α))
2. Sigmoidal response to steering magnitude
3. **Phase boundaries:** Sudden behavioral shifts when ICL and steering interact
4. **Additivity:** Both interventions combine linearly in log-belief space

---

### c) Key Results

**Figure 1 (Overview):** Conceptual diagram showing ICL and steering both move belief state from neutral persona c' to target persona c, but via different mechanisms (likelihood vs prior).

**Figure 2 (Replication):** Replicates Anil et al. (2024) many-shot ICL results on persona-matching (Machiavellianism, Psychopathy, Narcissism, etc.). Behavior improves sigmoidally with log(N) shots.

**Figure 3 (Belief updating):** Representational + Bayesian perspectives. ICL shifts belief along concept vector via evidence; steering shifts via prior manipulation.

**Figure 4 (ICL dynamics):** The key prediction—ICL follows sigmoid as function of N^(1-α), not log(N). The sub-linear scaling (α ≈ 0.5-0.7) explains why many-shot ICL shows sudden improvement. High-prior concepts plateau earlier.

**Figure 5 (Steering response):** Steering magnitude m produces sigmoidal behavior change. Range [-1,1] is approximately linear; beyond that, saturation effects occur.

**Figure 6 (Phase diagram):** **Main result.** Heatmaps showing behavior as joint function of ICL shots and steering magnitude. A phase boundary emerges—behavior suddenly shifts when crossing a threshold. The Bayesian model accurately reconstructs this.

**Figure 7 (Additional tasks):** Replicates on high-prior concepts (Openness) and flipped-label sentiment detection.

---

### d) Methods & Takeaway

**Methods:**
- **CAA (Contrastive Activation Addition):** Steering vectors computed as difference in mean activations between contrasting datasets (e.g., harmful vs harmless prompts)
- **Many-shot ICL:** Up to 128 in-context examples
- **Models tested:** Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen-2.5-7B, Gemma-2-9B, Phi-4-mini-instruct, Llama-3.1-70B
- **Datasets:** Persona matching (Dark Triad traits), sentiment detection, code completion

**Key caveats:**
- Only binary concepts tested
- CAA is one steering method; SAE-based steering may differ
- Steering breaks down at very large magnitudes (Fig. 13) — representation space has limits

**Takeaway:** ICL and steering are not separate hacks—they're dual aspects of Bayesian belief updating. This means practitioners can predictably combine them, and safety researchers should watch for phase transitions where small changes in prompts or steering induce large behavioral shifts.

## Paper 2: Acetylcholine demixes heterogeneous dopamine signals for learning and moving

**Authors:** (Nature Neuroscience, 2026)  
**Venue:** Nature Neuroscience  
**Link:** https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-026-02227-x  
**Note:** [[2026/Acetylcholine demixes heterogeneous dopamine signals for learning and moving]]  
**Access:** ⚠️ Paywalled — abstract + partial text only

---

### a) Introduction

Dopamine neurons in the striatum are implicated in both reinforcement learning (via reward prediction errors, RPEs) and motor control. The puzzle: how can the same dopamine signals serve both learning and movement? Some evidence suggests distinct dopamine neuron populations, but single axon terminals span millimeters, and heterogeneous signals are observed at single recording sites. The authors propose that **acetylcholine (ACh) dynamically demixes these multiplexed signals**—determining moment-by-moment whether dopamine drives learning or movement.

**Why it matters:** Resolves a long-standing puzzle about dopamine heterogeneity; has implications for Parkinson's (where both learning and movement are impaired) and striatal circuit function.

---

### b) Experiment

**Task:** Self-paced temporal wagering task in rats
- Rats initiate trials by nose-poking a central port
- Auditory cue indicates reward magnitude (5-80 μl water)
- After fixation period, reward assigned randomly to left/right port
- Rats can wait for unpredictable delay or opt out
- Catch trials (15-25%) assess valuation via willingness-to-wait

**Manipulations:**
- Blocks of low vs high reward offers (to manipulate reward expectations)
- Fiber photometry for dopamine and ACh recordings in dorsomedial striatum (DMS)

---

### c) Results (Figure by Figure)

**Figure 1 (Task structure):**
- Rats initiate faster in high-reward blocks (Fig. 1c)
- Wait longer for larger rewards (Fig. 1d)
- Head tracking with DeepLabCut shows orienting movements (Fig. 1e-f)

**Figure 2-3 (Dopamine heterogeneity):**
- Dopamine encodes RPEs at reward-predictive cue offset
- Dopamine also predicts upcoming movement vigor (orienting movements)
- These signals occur at distinct time points but at the same recording sites

**Figure 4 (ACh dynamics):**
- **Key finding:** ACh dips during RPE-related dopamine signals
- ACh does NOT dip during movement-related dopamine signals
- ACh bursts during contralateral orienting movements

**Figure 5-6 (Causal role of ACh):**
- Dopamine RPEs predict subsequent behavior ONLY when they slightly lag ACh dips
- When dopamine RPEs precede ACh dips, no behavioral effect
- This suggests ACh "gates" dopamine's role in learning

**Figure 7 (Model):**
- ACh dynamics determine whether dopamine drives plasticity (learning) or movement vigor
- Moment-by-moment gating, not static neuron-type segregation

---

### d) Important Methods & Highlighted Points

**Methods:**
- **Fiber photometry:** Simultaneous dopamine and ACh recordings in DMS
- **Temporal wagering task:** Separates reward valuation from movement initiation
- **Trial-level analysis:** Correlates dopamine/ACh dynamics with behavior on individual trials

**Key points:**
- **Multiplexed signals:** Same dopamine axons carry both learning and movement signals
- **ACh as temporal filter:** Dips = learning mode; bursts = movement mode
- **Behavioral gating:** ACh timing determines whether dopamine RPEs update behavior
- **Parkinson's implication:** Both ACh and dopamine are affected in PD—this may explain why both learning and movement are impaired

**Takeaway:** Acetylcholine doesn't just modulate dopamine—it **demixes** it. The same dopamine signals serve different functions depending on ACh state. This reframes how we think about striatal circuit computation.

## Paper 3: Interpretability Illusions in the Generalization of Simplified Models

**Authors:** Dan Friedman, Aleksander Mądry, Nicholas Carlini  
**Venue:** ICML 2024  
**Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.03656  
**Note:** [[2026/causality, prediction in neuro, ai]]  
**Access:** ✅ Full PDF available on arXiv

---

### a) Introduction

A common interpretability method is to simplify models—e.g., SVD on hidden states, clustering activations—and then analyze the simplified representation. The implicit assumption: if the simplified model approximates the full model on the training set, it's faithful. This paper shows this assumption is **dangerously wrong**: simplified proxies can be faithful in-distribution but fail on out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization.

**Why it matters:** Most interpretability work uses simplification (probes, SVD, clustering). If these create illusions of understanding, we're building on shaky ground—especially for safety-critical applications.

---

### b) Main Contribution

The authors systematically test whether simplified models (SVD, clustering, linear probes) faithfully capture the full model's behavior on OOD tasks. They find:

1. **Generalization gaps are consistent:** Simplified proxies often diverge from the original model on systematic generalization tests
2. **Bidirectional failures:** Sometimes the original model generalizes but the simplified proxy fails; sometimes the opposite
3. **The illusion:** In-distribution faithfulness does not imply OOD faithfulness

---

### c) Key Results

**Setup:** Train Transformers on tasks with systematic generalization splits:
- **Dyck languages:** Balanced parentheses (test on deeper nesting than training)
- **Code completion:** Predict next token in Python-like code (test on longer sequences, new patterns)

**Simplification methods tested:**
- SVD on hidden states (low-rank approximation)
- K-means clustering of activations
- Linear probes for task-relevant features

**Figure 1-2 (Dyck language):**
- Full Transformer generalizes to deeper nesting
- SVD-reduced model (keeping 90% variance) fails on deeper nesting
- Clustering-based proxy also fails
- **But:** In-distribution, all proxies match the original perfectly

**Figure 3-4 (Code completion):**
- Similar pattern: proxies match in-distribution, diverge OOD
- In some cases, simplified proxy generalizes BETTER than original (rare but real)

**Figure 5 (Systematic analysis):**
- Generalization gap correlates with task complexity
- More complex tasks → larger gaps
- Gap exists even with high-variance SVD (keeping 95%+ of variance)

---

### d) Methods & Takeaway

**Methods:**
- **Controlled datasets:** Dyck languages with explicit depth splits; synthetic code with length/pattern splits
- **Faithfulness metric:** Behavioral equivalence—does simplified model make same predictions as original?
- **Multiple simplification methods:** SVD, clustering, probes

**Key caveats:**
- Only Transformer architectures tested
- Only algorithmic tasks (Dyck, code)—natural language may differ
- Simplification is necessary for human interpretability; the paper doesn't suggest abandoning it, but being cautious

**Takeaway:** **In-distribution faithfulness is not enough.** When evaluating interpretability methods, we must test OOD generalization. Otherwise, we risk "interpretability illusions"—thinking we understand a model when we only understand its training distribution behavior.
